Most advice about morning routines asks for a lot. Wake at five. Meditate, stretch, read, plan, take a cold shower, write a gratitude list. It sounds lovely on paper, and it tends to last about four days — until one slow morning breaks the chain and the whole thing quietly falls away.

If that has happened to you, the problem probably wasn't discipline. It was size. A routine that asks for an hour has to win against everything else a morning holds. So let's start with one that asks for almost nothing.

## The whole routine: coffee and one line

Here it is. Make yourself a coffee — or tea, or warm water with lemon; the drink is up to you. While it's still hot, sit down with a notebook and a pen, and write one line by hand.

That's it. One line. Not a page, not three pages. A sentence about yesterday, a thought about today, something you noticed on the way to the kettle. *Want to finish the draft before lunch.* *The cat was being ridiculous again.* Or simply:

>> Slept badly, but the light is nice.

Then close the notebook and get on with your day.

## Why something this small works

**It's too small to skip.** On a good morning, you'll write more. On a hard morning, one line still takes less than a minute. A routine that survives your worst days is the only kind that becomes a routine at all.

**It's anchored to something you already do.** You were going to make coffee anyway. Pairing a new habit with an existing one means you don't have to remember to start. The coffee reminds you.

**Handwriting slows you down, in a good way.** Typing is fast and a little frantic, and there's always a notification one tab away. A pen moves at the speed of thought, maybe a bit slower. For that one minute you're doing just one thing, which is a rare and quiet feeling first thing in the morning.

**It gives you a small win before the day asks anything of you.** Before emails, before messages, before the to-do list, you've already done the thing you set out to do. It's a tiny start, but it's yours.

**The lines add up.** After a month you'll have thirty sentences. Flip back through them and you'll find a small, honest diary of how your days actually went — what worried you, what you were looking forward to, what turned out fine. It's surprisingly lovely to read.

> **Try this tomorrow:** Put a notebook and a pen next to your kettle or coffee machine tonight. When you make your first drink, write one line before your first sip. Nothing else.

## Letting it grow, slowly

Once the one-line morning feels automatic — give it two or three weeks, and don't rush — you can let it grow. Add one small thing at a time, and only when the step before feels easy.

- **After a few weeks:** below your line, write the one thing you'd most like to get done today. Just one.
- **A little later:** drink your coffee somewhere with a window, and look outside for a minute before you pick up your phone.
- **Later still:** add five minutes for your body — a short walk, a few stretches, or simply opening the window and breathing properly.

Some people find their routine settles at ten minutes. Some grow it to forty. Both are fine. The point isn't to reach a particular shape; it's to have a start to the day that feels like yours.

## When you miss a day

You will miss days. Everyone does. Travel, a sick child, an early train. The trick is not to treat a missed morning as a broken streak — there's no streak here to break. Just write one line the next time you make coffee. The notebook doesn't mind the gap.

And if the routine ever starts to feel like a chore, shrink it back down. One line is always enough.

## A quiet way into the day

When the coffee is finished and the line is written, the rest of your morning is waiting. If your first task needs real focus, you're welcome to bring it to [the room](/) — put on a little music, start a timer, and work quietly alongside others doing the same.
