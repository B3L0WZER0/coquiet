There are days when music helps everything move. And there are days when even a piano feels like one more thing asking for your attention.

Silence is not always quiet, either. In silence, you notice the conversation across the room, the traffic outside, the fridge switching on — and every thought that has been waiting for an empty moment.

This is where ambient sound can help. It gives the room a steady texture. Sudden noises have somewhere to disappear, and your mind has less reason to turn towards every little change.

Not because the sound makes you focus. It simply makes focusing feel a little easier.

## Why sounds have colours

In acoustics, colour names describe how a sound is spread across different frequencies. White noise is the familiar example: a broad wash of sound, often compared to radio static.

But outside a laboratory, people use these colours a little more loosely. They become a way to describe texture and mood — whether a sound feels bright, deep, soft, open or alive.

In Coquiet, the colours are three different sound worlds: coastal waves, a forest, and snowfall. You do not need to understand the science to choose one. You only need to notice how each one makes the room feel.

## Blue: when you need a little movement

Blue is the sound of coastal waves on a pebble shore.

There is movement in it, but no melody to follow. A wave arrives, draws back, and makes room for the next one. That gentle repetition can be useful when silence feels flat or when you need a little momentum to begin.

Water sounds can also make a space feel wider. Even at a desk, there is the sense of looking beyond the room for a while.

Try Blue for writing, planning, or the first half-hour of a task you have been putting off.

## Green: when the room feels too small

Green is forest birdsong, with wind moving through the leaves.

It is the most alive of the three. There are small changes to notice — a bird further away, a branch shifting, a quieter moment between them — but nothing asks for an answer.

Natural soundscapes are often associated with restoration and a better mood. Perhaps part of that is simply the feeling of being somewhere with air and distance, rather than inside another tab.

Try Green when you have been at the screen for a while, or when you want company that feels light and awake.

If birdsong catches your attention too often, that is useful to know. Another sound may suit the task better.

## White: when you want almost nothing to happen

White is slow snowfall over a quiet meadow: soft wind, a frosted tree, very little else.

It is the palest and least eventful of the three. There is no clear rhythm and not much to anticipate. It simply stays with you.

That makes it a good place for reading, careful work, or moments when your mind already feels full. It can soften the edges of the sounds around you without replacing them with something busy.

Try White when you want the background to become a background again.

## Choose with your attention, not the label

The same sound will not help everyone, or even help the same person every day. A forest may feel restorative on Tuesday and distracting on Thursday. Waves may carry one task along and make another feel restless.

Keep the volume lower than you think. Let the sound sit behind the work rather than on top of it. Then give it five minutes before deciding.

> **Try this next session:** Begin with the sound that matches how you want to feel — Blue for movement, Green for space, White for softness. After five minutes, ask only: am I thinking about the sound, or has it begun to disappear?

The best ambient sound is usually the one you stop noticing.
