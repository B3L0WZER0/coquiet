Some tasks fit neatly into an afternoon. You start, work for a while, and reach the end before it is time to stop.

Most worthwhile things are not like that.

A draft takes several sittings. A project follows you through the week. You close the document on Friday and open it again on Monday, only to find that the person who understood what was happening has disappeared.

You read the last few paragraphs. Open three old tabs. Look through your notes. Ten minutes pass while you slowly become yourself again.

There is a gentler way to leave unfinished work.

## Stop while you still know what comes next

When we have been working for a while, there is a temptation to keep going until we run out of energy or time. Then we close everything at the exact moment the path becomes unclear.

If you can, stop a little earlier.

Leave when you still know the next sentence, the next calculation, or the next small decision. You are not wasting momentum. You are saving a little of it for the person who will return.

It is easier to begin with a step waiting than with an empty space.

## Leave a note for your future self

Before you close the task, write down where you stopped.

Keep it ordinary and specific:

>> The introduction is finished. Next, add the three examples to the second section. The numbers are in the September spreadsheet.

That is more useful than *continue report*. It tells you what has already been decided, what needs to happen next, and where the pieces are.

You can leave the note at the top of the document, on a piece of paper, or wherever you will see it first. Its job is not to organise the whole project. Its job is to save you from having to reconstruct yesterday.

## Make the next action visible

A half-finished task often feels larger after it has been left alone. “Finish the presentation” can grow into something heavy over the weekend.

Look for the first physical action instead.

Open the feedback. Choose the final photograph. Write the heading for the last section. Put the two figures beside each other and compare them.

The action should be small enough that you can do it before you have time to wonder whether you feel ready.

Readiness often arrives a few minutes after you begin.

> **Try this before you stop:** Leave three short lines: “I finished…”, “Next, I will…”, and “This can wait…” Then close the task without carrying the rest of it with you.

## Clear the edges, not the evidence

You do not need to leave your desk perfectly tidy. Sometimes the open book, the marked-up page, or the tool beside the unfinished piece is exactly what helps you remember.

Clear away what no longer belongs to the task. Keep the few things that show you where to return.

The aim is not a beautiful workspace. It is a workspace that remembers.

The same is true on a screen. Close the tabs you opened by accident. Keep the document, reference, and note you will need first. Give tomorrow a quieter starting point.

## Begin with the note

When you come back, resist the urge to review the entire project before doing anything.

Read the note you left. Take the small action. Let the wider context return while your hands are already moving.

If the note no longer makes sense, write a better one before you go further. That, too, is progress. You are learning what your future self needs from you.

## Unfinished can still be safely put down

Leaving work well is a small form of trust.

You do not have to keep rehearsing the task so you will remember it. You do not have to solve the next part while making dinner or trying to sleep. The work has a place, and the next step is waiting there.

When you are ready to return, [the room](/) is open. Bring the note you left yourself, choose a little music or ambient sound, and begin with the next small thing.

You already made it easier to come back.
