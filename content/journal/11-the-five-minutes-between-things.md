A meeting finishes at 10:59. At 11:00, you open the document you meant to work on.

Your hands have moved on, but part of your mind is still in the conversation. You remember something you should have said. Wonder whether you promised to send a link. Replay a sentence that sounded different after it left your mouth.

Meanwhile, the new task is already asking you to think.

Most days are full of these small crossings: from email to writing, from a call to lunch, from one problem to another. We often treat the minutes between them as wasted space and try to remove them.

But a little space can be useful.

## Let one thing finish

Before opening the next tab, give the last task a clear ending.

Write down anything you need to remember. Send the short follow-up if it will take less than a minute. Leave a note about the next step. Then close what you can.

You are not trying to make the task perfectly complete. You are giving it somewhere to stay while you are elsewhere.

A simple sentence is often enough:

>> Waiting for Sam’s answer. Look again on Thursday.

Once the thought has a place, your mind does not have to keep holding it in the doorway.

## Change something physical

Stand up. Refill your glass. Open the window for a moment. Walk to the other side of the room and back.

The movement does not need to count as exercise. It simply tells your body that one part of the day has ended and another is beginning.

If you work in different places, let the walk be the pause. If you stay at the same desk, change one small thing: move the finished papers aside, turn to a fresh page, or switch the sound in the room.

A physical change makes the transition easier to feel.

## Do not fill the gap immediately

Five spare minutes can look like an invitation to check something.

You open your messages, read two headlines, or look at the weather. None of it takes long. But now you are carrying one more conversation, one more idea, and perhaps a small amount of news you cannot do anything about.

Leave some gaps unfilled.

Look out of the window. Let the kettle boil without taking your phone with you. Sit for a moment before deciding what the next moment is for.

Boredom is allowed to be brief.

> **Try this between two tasks:** Write one line to close the first thing. Stand up and take ten slow steps. Sit down, name the next thing, and begin before opening anything else.

## Arrive before you organise

When you return, you may feel tempted to prepare extensively: rearrange the desk, choose the right music, review the whole plan.

Try beginning with one small action instead.

Read the first paragraph. Put the two files beside each other. Write the heading. Give the next task two quiet minutes before deciding how difficult it feels.

Sometimes resistance belongs to the transition, not the work itself.

## When there are not five minutes

Some days, the next call has already started. Someone needs an answer. The afternoon is a row of things with no space between them.

Make the pause smaller.

Exhale before you click Join. Put both feet on the floor. Close the old document before opening the new one. Say, quietly if you like, *That is finished for now. This is what I am doing next.*

Ten seconds can still be a threshold.

## A quieter way through the day

A pause will not make every task easy. It will not give you more hours or stop the unexpected from arriving.

It can keep the day from becoming one long, blurred thing.

You finish. You move. You arrive.

And when you are ready for the next stretch of work, [the room](/) is open. Choose a little music or ambient sound, set the timer, and give this task its own beginning.
