The important tasks are often the hardest to begin.

A message you care about sits unanswered. A blank document waits for the idea you can already imagine clearly. The presentation matters, so the first slide somehow has to prove that the whole thing will be good.

You are not short of thoughts. You may simply be asking the beginning to do the work of the ending.

A first version does not need to be convincing. It only needs to exist.

## Making and judging are different jobs

When you make something and evaluate it at the same time, every sentence has to pass an examination before the next one can arrive.

You write a line, read it back, change two words, wonder whether the whole direction is wrong, and return to the beginning.

Judgement is useful. It helps you notice what is unclear, unnecessary or not quite true. But it works better when there is something in front of you to judge.

For a little while, let making go first.

## Make the smallest honest version

A rough version does not have to include everything.

Write the email in three plain sentences. Put the presentation headings on empty slides. Sketch the shape of the page without choosing colours. Explain the idea as though you were telling it to one patient person.

Do not try to sound polished. Try to make the thought visible.

You can add nuance once you know what the thing is.

## Leave the difficult parts open

Sometimes one missing detail stops the whole draft.

You cannot remember the number. The opening is not right. You need a better example, but none is coming.

Leave a space.

Write *[find the figure]*, *[say this more simply]* or *[example goes here]* and continue. A placeholder is not a failure. It is a small bridge over a part you are not ready to solve.

The rest of the work may show you what belongs there.

## Keep the first version private for a moment

Not every beginning needs an audience.

If you imagine someone reading each sentence while you write it, you may begin editing for their reaction before you have heard your own thought.

Give the work a short period in which it belongs only to you. Ten minutes can be enough. So can one untidy page.

You are allowed to make something that is not ready to explain itself.

> **Try this today:** Set a timer for ten minutes and make the most ordinary version you can. Use simple words. Leave gaps. Do not go back to the beginning until the timer ends.

## Return with a smaller question

When the first version exists, take a short break before improving it.

Then avoid asking, *Is this good?* The question is too large, and it rarely tells you what to do next.

Ask something you can act on:

* Is the main idea clear?
* What can be removed?
* Where would an example help?
* Does the order make sense?
* Which sentence sounds least like me?

One useful question turns judgement into work.

## Let improvement be gradual

The polished version may be several versions away.

That is not evidence that the first attempt was bad. It means the first attempt did its job: it gave the second one somewhere to begin.

A potter does not expect a finished bowl from a lump of clay. There is centring, shaping, trimming, drying and firing. Each stage has its own kind of attention.

Your work can have stages too.

When you are ready to make the first one, [the room](/) is open. Choose a little music or ambient sound, set the timer, and make something ordinary enough to improve.

You do not need to impress the blank page.
