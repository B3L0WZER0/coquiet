There is the work itself: the paragraph, the sketch, the difficult reply, the first clean line of code.

And then there is the work before the work.

Finding the right file. Clearing yesterday's glass from the table. Plugging in the headphones. Closing the windows you no longer need. Deciding, in plain words, what you are here to do.

None of this looks important. But it changes the shape of the beginning.

## The doorway is often the hardest part

A task can be perfectly manageable once you are inside it. The trouble is crossing the threshold.

At the beginning, every small question is still open. Where should I start? Which version is current? Do I need to answer that message first? Would a different playlist help? Perhaps I should make a better plan.

Each question is tiny. Together, they can keep you circling the work without ever touching it.

Preparation closes a few of those questions in advance. It places the oars in the boat, so to speak. When the moment comes to begin, there is less to negotiate with.

## Make a runway, not a ritual

This does not need to become an elaborate morning routine.

The point is not to arrange the perfect conditions. Perfect conditions are another form of waiting. The point is simply to remove one or two pieces of friction between you and the first honest action.

If you want to write, leave the document open at the sentence that needs you. If you want to read, put the book where your hand will find it. If you have a difficult task tomorrow, write its first small step before you finish today.

A runway can be very short. It only needs to point in one direction.

## Prepare for the person you will actually be

We often set up our work for an imaginary version of ourselves: rested, decisive, undistracted and unusually enthusiastic.

But Monday morning may bring a slower person. A person who slept badly, has three things on their mind and needs a little help finding the thread.

Prepare for that person instead.

Give them a task small enough to enter. Leave them a useful note. Put the necessary things together. Choose the sound before they need it. Be specific about what *done for now* will mean.

This is not lowering the standard. It is making the standard reachable.

## Do not turn preparation into hiding

There is a moment when getting ready stops helping and becomes a gentler way of avoiding the work.

You reorganise the folder. Then rename the files. Then search for a nicer tool. Everything feels adjacent to progress, but the real task remains untouched.

A useful preparation should make the next action obvious. Once it does, it is finished.

You do not need the whole lake to be clear. You only need the rope untangled and enough room to push away from shore.

> **Try this before your next focus session:** Take one quiet minute. Put away what belongs to the last task. Open only what the next task needs. Then write one sentence: “For the next 25 minutes, I am here to…” Finish the sentence, choose your sound, and begin.

## A kinder beginning

The work before the work is easy to dismiss because it produces nothing you can point to. No finished page. No checked box. No visible result.

What it produces is a beginning with less resistance.

That matters. Not because every session must be effortless, but because attention is easier to offer when it does not first have to fight through clutter, choices and uncertainty.

So before you ask yourself to focus, take a moment to make focus possible. Clear a small space. Name the next step. Let the room grow quiet around one thing.

Then set out.

When you are ready, [join the room](/) and begin alongside others who are doing the same—quietly, in their own corner of the day.
